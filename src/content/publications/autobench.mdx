Every few months a new language model tops a leaderboard, and every few months someone asks whether the number still means anything. Part of the doubt is practical. A benchmark that is public for long enough can leak into training data, and a test that every strong model passes no longer separates the strong from the stronger. Part of it is structural, because each fix we know has moved the dependency somewhere else: toward human annotators who write the tasks, toward a fixed panel of judge models, or toward one judge, e.g., GPT-4, that is trusted by default.

AutoBench asks what happens if no outside party is needed. The models being ranked write the tasks, answer them, and grade each other, and the grades of models that prove reliable count for more. This is a form of peer review between language models, and the question we cared about is whether a ranking obtained that way tells us anything that established benchmarks do not contradict.

## Why LLM-as-a-judge needs more than one judge

Using a language model to grade another is now routine, and it is cheap, but a single judge brings its own habits into every score. A model that grades answers may also favor its own. The judgment matrix in the paper makes this inspectable, since its diagonal records how each model scores itself, so self-preference is a visible quantity and not an assumption. Spreading the grading over every participant dilutes the bias of any one judge.

In practice, this is multi-judge LLM evaluation with a feedback loop, i.e., a model earns authority as a grader through its standing as a contestant. The structure is close to peer review, where authors also act as reviewers of one another. Whether that intuition transfers to language models is what the experiment below tests.

## Evaluating LLMs without human labels

The design removes human labels from the loop entirely. There is no answer key, and there is no annotator. Tasks come from the models themselves, filtered by a quality check that the same weighted panel applies, and answers are graded on a 1 to 5 scale with a strict rule against rewarding factual errors. This matters for anyone who needs to compare models in a domain where no public benchmark exists, since the task categories form a list that can be extended by naming a new category and letting the models write the tasks.

Because fresh tasks are drawn every iteration, the benchmark is dynamic in the sense that a dataset cannot be memorized ahead of time. As such, we argue that it resists benchmark contamination better than a fixed test set, a claim that follows from how the tasks are made.

## How well peer rankings agree with MMLU-Pro and GPQA

We ran twelve open-source models through 40 iterations and compared the resulting order against two established benchmarks.

| Benchmark | Multi-judge tau | Multi-judge rho | Single-judge tau | Single-judge rho |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| MMLU-Pro | 0.64 | 0.78 | 0.45 | 0.61 |
| GPQA | 0.52 | 0.63 | 0.45 | 0.59 |

A Spearman's rho of 0.78 with MMLU-Pro comes from a procedure with no fixed answer key, and the ranking also tracks GPQA, a graduate-level question set, which suggests the signal reaches beyond recall of facts. The multi-judge pipeline beat a variant judged only by gpt-oss-20b, the best of the twelve, on both benchmarks, with the larger gap on MMLU-Pro. The weights of the full panel also settle quickly over the iterations, whereas the single-judge run converges less smoothly.

These are rank correlations over twelve models, and they show that the peer ranking is consistent with other benchmarks, not that it equals a human verdict. Direct human evaluation remains the reference for qualities such as creativity and subtle reasoning, and we describe AutoBench as a complement to it.

## Who can use a dynamic, self-sustaining LLM benchmark

A team that fine-tunes models for a narrow domain could add that domain as a category and track models on it without commissioning a test set. A group that tracks a field of fast-moving open models could rerun the loop whenever a new checkpoint appears. The public AutoBench project already operates at a larger scale than this study, with a third run over 33 models and about 300,000 individual ranks (Kruger, 2025a), and the paper provides the methodology and validation behind it.

The leaderboards reflect a consensus among models, not a definitive judgment of quality, safety or alignment, so they can inform a decision about which model to deploy but should not be its only basis.

## Where it goes next

The same loop can take in any model that can write, answer and judge, so the next panels include leading proprietary models alongside open-source ones. Pairing the peer rankings with large-scale human evaluation would then tie the consensus to human judgment directly, on tasks that no fixed benchmark covers.
