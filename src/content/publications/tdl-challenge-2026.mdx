Suppose a graph neural network (GNN) and a topological neural network (TNN) both claim to be good at spotting triangles in a network. The two papers use different datasets, different splits, and different code, so the comparison says little about which family is the better tool. The 2026 Topological Deep Learning Challenge set out to remove that excuse. It asked participants to implement recent architectures in TopoBench, the open-source benchmarking library for topological deep learning (TDL), and to run all of them through one evaluation pipeline on graphs whose structure is set by design.

This page is a companion to the report. It explains what made this edition different from [the 2025 one](/publications/tdl-challenge-2025/), what a GNN vs topological neural network benchmark can and cannot tell a practitioner, and what Gabriele Onorato and I submitted as team howyadoin, including the winner of Track 1.

## How the challenge worked

Participants chose one of two tracks. Track 1 covered GNNs, i.e., message passing on plain graphs, including graph transformers with structural encodings. Track 2 covered TNNs, which use higher-order message passing over simplicial complexes, cell complexes, hypergraphs, and combinatorial complexes. Every submission was a pull request against TopoBench that had to pass continuous integration, with tests, linting, and formatting checks.

The field was 34 teams and 45 registered participants, and the split between tracks was close to even (see the chart). A panel of TopoBench maintainers and collaborators decided the awards by Condorcet vote on four criteria: correctness, code quality, benchmarking on the GraphUniverse datasets, and documentation and tests. The criteria reward careful, reusable implementations, so every edition leaves TopoBench with more models that others can build on.

## Why synthetic graphs from GraphUniverse

Real benchmarks bundle many properties at once, so when a model wins it is hard to say why. GraphUniverse is a framework for sampling synthetic graphs, and here it let the organizers vary three properties independently: homophily (how often neighbors share a label), average degree, and how heavy-tailed the degree distribution is. Each GraphUniverse dataset is therefore a small controlled experiment, and the same grid of graphs is given to every model, whatever its domain. Concretely, homophily takes three levels, average degree two, and the degree-distribution exponent two, which gives 12 configurations with three training seeds each.

## What the comparison suggests

The report's main finding is that the difference between the tracks depends on the task. On community detection the mean accuracy gap is modest. On triangle counting, where the answer is a higher-order quantity by definition, the average error of Track 1 is 1.85 times that of Track 2. The report also finds that several structurally informed GNNs beat the Track 2 mean on triangle counting.

For someone choosing a model, both results are useful. The triangle-counting gap is consistent with the case for encoding higher-order structure, whereas the structurally informed GNNs show that a graph model with good structural features is a serious contender. The natural next step is real data, where homophily, degree, and tail weight come tangled together.

## My contribution

Gabriele Onorato and I competed as team howyadoin and submitted five models, all from the sheaf family: three in Track 1 and two in Track 2. Our implementation of the Directed Sheaf Neural Network (DSNN, from Fiorini et al., ICLR 2026) took **first place in Track 1 (Graph Neural Networks)**, and the team received the honorable mention "Excellent portfolio", given to two teams for "multiple high-quality contributions across both tracks".

DSNN ([pull request #412](https://github.com/geometric-intelligence/topobench/pull/412)) is a directed sheaf neural network implementation in PyTorch. Its restriction maps carry a phase read off the adjacency, which gives a Hermitian directed sheaf Laplacian that reduces to the ordinary one on undirected graphs. We implemented it with a real-valued lifting, so no complex tensor is ever materialized. The pull request carries 223 unit tests that check the paper's theorems rather than tensor shapes, full line coverage of its new files, API documentation, and a notebook that reproduces the paper's directed stochastic block model figure, where only edge directions reveal the communities. For readers who care about speed, our own work on sheaf networks is in [Implicit Sheaf Operators](/publications/implicit-sheaf-operators/).

The other four models are Sheaf Attention Networks ([#322](https://github.com/geometric-intelligence/topobench/pull/322)) and Neural Sheaf Propagation ([#326](https://github.com/geometric-intelligence/topobench/pull/326)) in Track 1, and the Copresheaf Topological Neural Network ([#405](https://github.com/geometric-intelligence/topobench/pull/405)) and the Directional Sheaf Hypergraph Network ([#408](https://github.com/geometric-intelligence/topobench/pull/408)) in Track 2. Across the five pull requests we wrote more than 400 unit tests; for DSNN, the copresheaf network, and the hypergraph network, they check the equations and theorems of the original papers directly.
