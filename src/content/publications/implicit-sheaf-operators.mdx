Graph neural networks (GNNs) that average over neighbors run into trouble in two familiar places: heterophilous graphs, where connected nodes tend to carry different labels, and deep stacks of layers, where node representations collapse toward each other (oversmoothing). Sheaf Neural Networks (SNNs) address both by letting every edge learn how the features of its two endpoints should be translated before they are compared, and Neural Sheaf Diffusion (NSD; Bodnar et al., 2022) is the best-known recipe for doing so. What stops many teams from trying it is cost. The standard implementation builds a sparse sheaf Laplacian with *nd* rows and columns, where *n* is the number of nodes and *d* the stalk dimension, so a graph of 1,000 nodes with 8-dimensional stalks already yields an 8,000 by 8,000 operator that must be assembled from learned maps.

This page is a companion to the paper. It covers where that cost comes from, what Implicit Sheaf Operators (ISOs) change in practice, and what we think the results make possible for efficient sheaf neural networks.

## Why sheaf neural networks are expensive

In a GCN the propagation matrix is fixed by the graph, so it is built once. In an SNN each edge carries learned *d* by *d* restriction maps, which means the operator depends on the weights and has to be re-assembled at every gradient step, and again for every new input graph in inductive settings such as classifying many small molecules or networks. Assembly ends with sorting and merging the sparse entries, and the operator then sits in memory for the backward pass. In our measurements on random graphs with 1,000 nodes, this made NSD up to 39.2 times slower and 9.27 times more memory hungry than a GCN, a gap that matters most on constrained hardware.

## A faster way to compute Neural Sheaf Diffusion and sheaf convolution

ISOs are a faster way to compute Neural Sheaf Diffusion, and they apply equally to the sheaf convolution we introduce below. They are matrix-free: instead of materializing the sheaf Laplacian, an ISO evaluates its action node by node from the restriction maps, using two batched matrix multiplications per edge and a scatter-add. This is ordinary gather-scatter message passing. The result equals multiplying by the explicit Laplacian, thus a model keeps the same expressiveness and only the way the operator is applied changes. The paper works one case end to end: three agents on a path, each holding a 2-dimensional measurement, reaching consensus through a local update at the middle node. Distributed coordination over cellular sheaves (Hanks et al., 2025) is a natural fit for this local formulation, although we did not test it.

## Comparison with connection Laplacians and other sheaf neural networks

Every sheaf variant we know of in this line pays for the same explicit block matrix. Connection Laplacians (Barbero et al., 2022a) build it once, ahead of training, which helps only when the graph never changes. Neural Sheaf Propagation (Suk et al., 2022), Sheaf Attention Networks (Barbero et al., 2022b) and nonlinear sheaf diffusion (Zaghen et al., 2024) alter what happens inside the diffusion step, yet still assemble that matrix and inherit its time, memory and power cost. Deep Neural Sheaf Diffusion (Bourgerie et al., 2026) points to the adjacency form for deeper SNNs. ISOs target the shared bottleneck itself, so in our view they complement these designs: maps are still learned end to end, and the graph can differ from one input to the next.

## Speed, memory, and what the numbers mean

On random graphs with 1,000 nodes, the best forward-pass speedup against an optimized explicit baseline is 4.87 times (37.78 ms down to 7.76 ms), and peak GPU memory falls up to 3.92 times (1816 MB to 463 MB). The two gains peak in different places. For general and bundle maps, time savings are largest for small stalks (the plot below), whereas memory savings grow with the stalk dimension and reach their maximum at *d* = 8. In practice, richer stalks, which give a sheaf its modeling power, become cheaper to afford. The gains matter most in inductive workloads, since a transductive model pays the assembly cost only once.

## Sheaf neural networks on edge devices

We also ran graph classification inference on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano, an embedded GPU board, using the PTC and Proteins datasets. Energy per graph dropped by up to 1.24 times and on-device memory by up to 3.08 times. The energy gain is modest and the memory gain is substantial, so we read this as a first sign that sheaf models can fit the budgets of on-board and low-power deployments.

## Sheaf Convolutional Networks for heterophily

Keeping only the adjacency term of the Laplacian, as GCNs do relative to graph diffusion, yields Sheaf Convolutional Networks (SCNs), evaluated by ISOs at the same cost. On the heterophily-oriented node classification benchmark of Pei et al. (2020), SCNs beat NSD variants by up to 3.93 points; on Squirrel, a general-map SCN reaches 60.27 against 56.34 for the best NSD. Open questions remain, notably how far deeper SCNs can go (Bourgerie et al., 2026) and whether the matrix-free idea carries over to copresheaf networks (Hajij et al., 2025), which still use explicit block matrices.
